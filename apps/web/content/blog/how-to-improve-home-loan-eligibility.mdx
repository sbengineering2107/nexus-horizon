## How do banks decide how much home loan I can get?

Two limits apply, and you get the lower of the two. The first is what your income can repay. The second is the maximum share of the property value that RBI allows the bank to lend. A strong credit score helps you reach the top of both limits. A weak one can pull you below them.

Lenders work it out in roughly this order:

1. Take your net monthly income and apply the lender's limit on how much of it can go towards EMIs.
2. Subtract the EMIs you already pay (car, personal, education loans and card dues).
3. Convert the remaining EMI into a loan amount using the tenure and interest rate.
4. Cap the result at the loan-to-value (LTV) limit for that property.
5. Adjust for credit score, age, employment and the property's legal status.

## What is FOIR and why does it matter so much?

FOIR, or fixed obligations to income ratio, is the share of your monthly income already committed to EMIs and other fixed payments. Lenders generally prefer total EMIs, including the new home loan, to stay below about 40–50% of income. The exact ceiling depends on the lender and your income level.

This is why existing loans hurt eligibility more than people expect. Every ₹10,000 of current EMI is ₹10,000 the bank will not allow on the home loan.

A common rule of thumb is that lenders offer up to about 60 times net monthly salary. For example, at ₹50,000 a month of take-home pay, that suggests around ₹30 lakh (roughly a 50% FOIR over 20 years at 8.5%). Treat this as a starting point, not a quote.

## How much of the property price will the bank fund?

RBI sets the maximum loan-to-value ratio by loan size:

| Loan amount | Maximum LTV | Minimum down payment |
|---|---|---|
| Up to ₹30 lakh | 90% | 10% |
| Above ₹30 lakh to ₹75 lakh | 80% | 20% |
| Above ₹75 lakh | 75% | 25% |

Stamp duty, registration and documentation charges are excluded from the property cost for this calculation, except for homes costing up to ₹10 lakh. So even if your income supports a larger loan, a ₹1 crore flat can be financed only up to ₹75 lakh. The rest, plus stamp duty and registration, comes from you.

## What else do lenders look at?

### Credit score

Lenders and banks describe a score of 750 or above as strong, and the best rates usually go to scores above 800. A lower score can mean the bank asks for a larger down payment, which cuts the loan even if your income is fine.

### Age and tenure

SBI's regular home loan allows a maximum age of 70 and tenure of up to 30 years. Other lenders set their own limits. Federal Bank, for example, lends to resident self-employed borrowers up to 65 years. The older you are when you apply, the shorter the tenure you can get, and the lower the loan your EMI supports.

### Employment and income documents

Salaried applicants typically need the last three months' salary slips and two years of Form 16 or income tax returns. Self-employed applicants usually need three years of returns, balance sheets and profit and loss accounts. Stable employment and steady income help. Frequent job changes or uneven business income can lower what the lender is willing to count.

### The property itself

Lenders check RERA registration, approved plans, title history and occupancy or completion documents. A property with legal gaps can be rejected or financed at a lower amount regardless of your income.

## How can I increase my home loan eligibility?

### Add an earning co-applicant

The lender adds a co-applicant's eligible income to yours. Banks typically accept a spouse, parent, son, unmarried daughter or sibling. The co-applicant's credit history is checked too, and both of you are liable for the full loan, so borrow on what the household could manage if one income dropped.

### Close small loans first

Clear personal loans, consumer-durable EMIs and credit card balances before applying. Business Standard suggests closing them four to six months ahead, as the closure can briefly affect your score, and avoiding new credit for about six months before applying. Collect a no-dues certificate for each closed loan.

### Choose a longer tenure

A longer tenure lowers the EMI, so the same income supports a larger loan. The trade-off is more interest over the life of the loan, but you can prepay or raise the EMI later as income grows. Several lenders, SBI among them, do not charge a prepayment penalty on home loans.

### Declare all regular income

Rental income, interest income and other steady receipts can be counted if you can document them, usually through tax returns and bank statements. Income that is not shown in returns is hard for a lender to accept.

### Consider a step-up loan

Some lenders offer step-up structures where EMIs start lower and rise over time. SBI's Flexipay home loan, for instance, is for salaried borrowers aged 21 to 45 and offers about 20% higher eligibility than its standard product, with an interest-only period at the start and stepped-up EMIs later. These suit people with secure, rising income. Higher later EMIs are a real risk if your income does not keep pace.

### Improve and check your credit report

Pay every EMI and card bill on time, keep card usage low, and check your report for errors before you apply. Apply to lenders selectively, since several applications at once can count against you.

## What lowers home loan eligibility?

- Existing EMIs and high credit card balances
- Late payments or defaults in your credit history
- Several loan or card applications in a short period
- Applying close to retirement age, which shortens the tenure
- Irregular or undocumented income
- A property with incomplete approvals or unclear title
